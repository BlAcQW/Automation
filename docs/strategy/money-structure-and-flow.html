Bookly · Technical brief

Money: structure & flow

How a customer's payment reaches a salon owner's Mobile Money, what holds the money in between, and the specific ways that can go wrong.

September 2026 · for the Bookly team and for Paystack · describes the code as built

01What changed, and why

Customers now pay into Bookly's Paystack account, not each salon's.

A salon owner never creates a payment gateway account or handles an API key. Text messages moved the same way, onto Bookly's Arkesel account.

The users are salon owners, barbers and nail technicians running a business from a phone. Asking them to open a Paystack account, find a secret key and paste it into a settings page is where they stop. So Bookly does it for them and settles up afterwards.

Why a wallet rather than Paystack's split payments. Splits would settle each salon's share directly and Bookly would hold nothing — simpler and lower risk. But splits are immutable and refunds come out of the platform balance with no clawback from the subaccount. Bookly sells deposits, which are conditional money, and chargebacks exist whatever the refund policy says. A wallet with a clearing step is the only shape that survives a customer disputing a charge after the salon has been paid.

Tenants who already connected their own Paystack keep using it. Money they are already collecting must never silently start landing somewhere else, so the collection route is recorded per payment and both paths run side by side.

02The flow

03The ledger

A balance is never a number anyone edits. It is the sum of an append-only log, and the cached columns exist only so a dashboard renders quickly — no withdrawal is ever authorised against them.

Accounts answer one question: who owns this money right now. They deliberately do not also track where it physically sits. Tracking both needs two-dimensional accounting, and the half-finished version of that is where sign errors hide.

AccountMeaning
EXTERNALOutside Bookly — the customer's card, the owner's MoMo wallet. Its existence is what lets an inbound payment sum to zero.
TENANT_PENDINGTheirs, but the job is not done. Not withdrawable.
TENANT_AVAILABLETheirs, withdrawable now.
PAYOUT_PENDINGCommitted to a transfer that has not confirmed.
PLATFORM_FEEBookly's revenue. A percentage in basis points, currently set to zero.

Every movement writes two or more rows that sum to zero, in one transaction. Money is moved, never created.

Amounts are integer minor units — pesewas — throughout. A float loses money to rounding and the loss is unrecoverable, because nobody can say when it happened.

04What happens to a deposit

Deposits are non-refundable. A customer who cannot make it reschedules, which keeps the same booking so their money simply travels with it — no ledger movement at all.

OutcomeWhere the money goes
Job completedReleased to the salon.
Customer no-showReleased to the salon. This is the entire point of taking a deposit.
Customer cancelsForfeit to the salon. That is what makes holding the slot worth anything.
Customer reschedulesTravels with the booking. Nothing moves.
Salon cancelsRefunded to the customer, automatically.
Why the last row is not a policy choice

Keeping a customer's money for work nobody is going to do is indefensible however the terms are written — and the alternative to refunding is not "we keep it", it is a chargeback that costs the deposit plus a dispute fee plus standing with Paystack. Money held with no owner and no exit is a leak, so the refund is automatic rather than a balance waiting for someone to notice.

05What stops it going wrong

RiskControl
Two withdrawals at onceBalance re-derived from the ledger inside a serializable transaction. Postgres aborts one of a colliding pair. Only one payout may be in flight at a time.
Overdrawing a balanceAfter every movement, balances are re-derived in the same transaction and a negative pot throws, rolling the write back. Conserving money per movement is not enough — two movements can each balance and still overdraw between them.
Refund and release collidingBoth take an atomic claim on the deposit before any provider call. One wins, the other stops.
Replayed webhooksEvery movement carries an idempotency key derived from the provider's reference, enforced by a unique index rather than an if that can lose a race.
Crediting money we never receivedThe collection route is recorded server-side when the link is created, and is the only thing allowed to decide whether a wallet may be credited.
UnderpaymentA payment short of what was asked does not confirm the booking and does not credit.
Paying twice on a timeoutOnly a definitive rejection returns the funds. An uncertain outcome leaves the payout in flight for the webhook to settle.
Account takeoverChanging the payout destination starts a 24-hour hold, is audited, and notifies the owner. Withdrawals are owner-only.
Marking work done that never happenedBooking transitions are a state machine, and a job cannot be completed before its start time.
Found by review, not by tests

An adversarial review of this path found a critical flaw: the collection route had been inferable from the transaction reference and the provider's metadata, both of which a tenant controls once they connect their own Paystack key. A tenant could have minted a payment in their own account and had Bookly credit and pay out real money. Every individual function behaved exactly as written, so no unit test would have caught it. It is fixed, and the inference path was deleted rather than patched.
